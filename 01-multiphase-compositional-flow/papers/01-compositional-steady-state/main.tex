\begin{abstract}
% <= 250 palavras, autocontido, sem referências (texto literal: a classe CAS grava o resumo num ficheiro auxiliar).
Steady-state compositional simulation of gas--liquid flow in wells, flowlines and risers couples a cubic equation of state and an isothermal flash to mixture momentum and energy balances closed by homogeneous or flow-pattern-dependent mechanistic relations. This article presents a simulator in which the physics is written once, in flux form, and executed on an array backend with automatic differentiation (JAX): the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes, the derivatives of the flash outputs with respect to pressure and temperature and the sparse Newton Jacobian of the nodal systems are exact, and the same compiled program is vectorised over many cases. Two flow models (no-slip and mechanistic with slip) are solved by three interchangeable methods, an adaptive Runge--Kutta march and finite-difference and finite-volume nodal systems, and are cross-verified on an offshore well--flowline--riser system to within 0.01 bar and 0.1 K, with global momentum and energy balances closed to machine precision by the finite-volume scheme. The simulator is compared with the reference implementation on 200 pressure-drop cases, the offshore system and a gas-condensate pipeline. The vectorised march then generates 300 training samples per segment in about one minute, and multilayer perceptrons trained with Adam reproduce outlet pressure and temperature with a coefficient of determination above 0.997; chained along the three segments they stay within 0.3\,\% of the physical model while evaluating in tens of microseconds, four orders of magnitude faster than the simulator. Finally, the axial position is added as an input so that the network returns whole profiles with the inlet condition imposed by construction, and a physics-informed variant penalises the residual of the governing equations at unsimulated collocation points, with the residual built from the simulator's own flux formulation and its gradient obtained without back-propagating through the iterative flash.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Flow assurance in offshore production depends on steady-state models of gas--liquid flow along wells, flowlines and risers that predict pressure, temperature, phase fractions and flow pattern from the fluid composition and the boundary conditions. The mechanistic, flow-pattern-based closures of \citet{taitel1976,taitel1980}, \citet{xiao1990} and \citet{gomez2000} remain the workhorse of pipeline design, and compositional formulations couple them to a cubic equation of state \citep{peng1976} and an isothermal flash \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b} so that phase behaviour, condensation and heat transfer are resolved along the pipe. \citet{carvalho2022} developed such a formulation with two flow models, a homogeneous no-slip model marched as an initial-value problem and a mechanistic model with slip solved as a nodal nonlinear system, validated it against a commercial simulator on 200 pressure-drop cases and two production systems, and trained neural networks as surrogate models of the physical model.

Two difficulties recur in this class of simulators. The first is numerical: the derivatives that drive both the march (the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes with respect to pressure and temperature, which contains the derivatives of the flash) and the Newton iterations of the nodal systems are usually approximated by finite differences, each of which costs additional flash calculations and introduces noise that interferes with convergence across phase changes. The second is cost: the flash dominates the run time, and the thousands of evaluations needed for optimisation, uncertainty quantification or surrogate training multiply it. Surrogate models \citep{hornik1989,kanin2019} address the second difficulty, and physics-informed neural networks \citep{raissi2019,karniadakis2021} promise surrogates that honour the governing equations with fewer simulated samples; but the latter require the residual of the governing equations to be evaluated and differentiated inside the training loop, which is not straightforward when the equations contain an iterative flash.

This article reports a reimplementation of the model of \citet{carvalho2022} designed around automatic differentiation and vectorisation \citep{jax2018}, and its use as a data generator and as a residual provider for neural surrogates. The contributions are: (i) a flux-form statement of both flow models on a single interface, with exact Jacobians and flash derivatives obtained by forward-mode automatic differentiation; (ii) three interchangeable solvers (adaptive march, finite differences, finite volumes) that verify each other on the same equations, with global balance diagnostics; (iii) a comparison with the reference implementation on three benchmark problems, which reproduces the reference where the physics is shared and isolates the discrepancies to their origin; (iv) a surrogate-model workflow in which the vectorised march generates the training data, the hyperparameter search of the reference is repeated in full and the surrogate replaces the physical model at a cost of microseconds; and (v) a physics-informed extension in which the axial position becomes an input of the network, the inlet condition is imposed by construction, and the residual of the governing equations, evaluated at unsimulated collocation points with the simulator's own closures, enters the loss with a gradient that avoids back-propagation through the flash.

Section~\ref{sec:model} states the model, Section~\ref{sec:numerics} the numerical methods and the differentiable implementation, Section~\ref{sec:results} the verification and validation, Section~\ref{sec:surrogate} the neural surrogates and Section~\ref{sec:pinn} the physics-informed extension.

\section{Mathematical model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Conservation equations in flux form}\label{sec:conservation}

A single pipe of length $L$ is described by segments of constant diameter $D$, roughness $\varepsilon$, inclination $\theta$, overall heat-transfer coefficient $U$ and ambient temperature $T_\mathrm{amb}$. The total mass flow rate $\mdot$ is constant; the vapour and liquid rates follow from the flash, $\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$ and $\mdot_L = (1 - w_G)\mdot$. Both flow models considered here are written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flux}
\frac{\dd \bPhi}{\dd x} = \bS, \qquad
\bPhi = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\mdot_G v_G + \mdot_L v_L}{A} + P \\[1.2ex] \dfrac{\mdot_G h_G + \mdot_L h_L}{\mdot} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\bS = \begin{pmatrix} -F - \rho_m g \sin\theta \\[0.6ex] \dfrac{Q_w}{\mdot} - g\sin\theta \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the first component is the mixture momentum balance (momentum flux plus pressure, with wall friction $F$ and the hydrostatic term with the in situ mixture density $\rho_m = \rho_G\alpha_G + \rho_L H_L$) and the second is the mixture energy balance in terms of the mass-weighted specific enthalpy, with the potential-energy term and the heat exchanged with the surroundings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qw}
Q_w = -\pi D\, U\, (T - T_\mathrm{amb}).
\end{equation}
The kinetic-energy contribution to the energy balance is neglected, as in the reference model; it is available as an option. The state vector is $\bu = (P, T)$: for a given composition and $\mdot$, every quantity in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:flux}--\eqref{eq:qw} is a local function of $\bu$ through the flash and the closure relations. The two models differ only in how the phase velocities and the friction term are closed.

\subsection{Thermodynamic model}\label{sec:thermo}

Phase equilibrium and phase properties come from the Peng--Robinson equation of state \citep{peng1976},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr}
P = \frac{RT}{v - b} - \frac{a\,\alpha(T)}{v(v + b) + b(v - b)},
\end{equation}
with the classical mixing rules and zero binary interaction parameters, as in the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:eos}). The isothermal flash solves the Rachford--Rice equation \citep{rachford1952} with successive substitution of the equilibrium ratios followed by Newton polishing \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b}, preceded by a stability test. Phase densities follow from the compressibility roots, enthalpies from the ideal-gas heat-capacity polynomials and the residual enthalpy of the equation of state, liquid viscosity from the corresponding-states correlation of \citet{allan1991}, vapour viscosity from \citet{pedersen1987} and surface tension from the parachor method; the parameters are those tabulated by \citet{carvalho2022}. The no-slip liquid volume fraction follows directly from the flash,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda}
\lambda_L = \frac{\mdot_L/\rho_L}{\mdot_L/\rho_L + \mdot_G/\rho_G}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closure relations}\label{sec:closures}

In the no-slip model the two phases share the velocity $v = \mdot/(\rho_m A)$ with $\rho_m = \rho_G(1-\lambda_L) + \rho_L\lambda_L$, and the friction term uses the Churchill friction factor \citep{churchill1977} with volume-weighted mixture density and viscosity. In the mechanistic model the flow pattern is identified by the Taitel--Dukler criteria \citep{taitel1976,taitel1980}, and the holdup and the friction term follow from the pattern-specific momentum balances of \citet{xiao1990} for stratified and annular flow and of \citet{gomez2000} for slug flow, with bubble flow treated by a drift-flux relation; the in situ holdup
\begin{equation}\label{eq:holdup}
H_L = \frac{S\lambda_L}{1 - \lambda_L + S\lambda_L}
\end{equation}
is expressed through the slip ratio $S = v_G/v_L$. The pattern map and the transitions are evaluated at every state, so that the pattern may change along the pipe.

\section{Numerical methods and differentiable implementation}\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Three solvers on one interface}\label{sec:solvers}

Because both models expose the same pair $(\bPhi, \bS)$ as functions of $\bu$, the solution methods are written once and apply to both. Three are used.

\paragraph{Marching} With $\bJ = \partial\bPhi/\partial\bu$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} becomes the initial-value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:march}
\bJ(\bu)\,\frac{\dd\bu}{\dd x} = \bS(\bu), \qquad \bu(0) = (P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}),
\end{equation}
integrated with the embedded Dormand--Prince 5(4) pair \citep{dormand1980} with adaptive step, from output point to output point, with the error measured on the state scaled by (\SI{1}{\bar}, \SI{1}{\kelvin}). The reference implementation expanded $\dd\bPhi/\dd x$ term by term, evaluated the partial derivatives of the flash outputs by one-sided finite differences at the cost of three flashes per evaluation, and integrated with a library solver; here $\bJ$ is exact (Section~\ref{sec:ad}) and a single flash is needed per right-hand-side evaluation. The converged equilibrium ratios of the last accepted step are carried as an auxiliary variable and seed the next flash, which halves the substitution iterations without changing the result.

\paragraph{Finite differences} With $P$ and $T$ as unknowns at $n$ nodes per segment, the flux derivative is discretised with the second-order operator $\mathbf{M}$ (centred inside, one-sided at the ends), $(\mathbf{M}\bPhi)_i = \bS_i$. For the mechanistic model this is the nodal method of the reference, with the phase mass rates eliminated algebraically ($\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$), which keeps two unknowns per node.

\paragraph{Finite volumes} Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} over each cell gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fv}
\bPhi_{i+1} - \bPhi_i = \frac{h}{2}\left(\bS_i + \bS_{i+1}\right),
\end{equation}
which is conservative by construction and never differentiates the fluxes across the kinks introduced by the dew point or by a change of flow pattern. Break points between segments are duplicated with continuity of $P$ and $T$. Both nodal systems are solved by Newton's method,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newton}
\bJ_N(\bu^{k})\,\delta\bu = -\mathbf{R}(\bu^{k}), \qquad \bu^{k+1} = \bu^{k} + \delta\bu,
\end{equation}
with the exact banded Jacobian assembled by forward-mode automatic differentiation with graph colouring (bandwidth two for the finite-difference operator, one for the finite volumes), starting from the no-slip march interpolated at the nodes. Every run reports the global balances $(\bPhi_\mathrm{out} - \bPhi_\mathrm{in}) - \int \bS\,\dd x$ per segment, evaluated on the solver's own grid, as a diagnostic.

\subsection{Automatic differentiation and vectorisation}\label{sec:ad}

The physics is written once against an injected array backend; the same code runs in NumPy, for inspection and small cases, and in JAX \citep{jax2018}, where it is compiled just in time and transformed. Three transformations matter here. First, forward-mode linearisation gives $\bJ$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:march} and the coloured Newton Jacobian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:newton} exactly, at the cost of two tangent evaluations instead of three extra flashes. Second, the flash is itself differentiable: the successive-substitution iterations are excluded from the derivative graph and the Newton polishing steps, which solve the equilibrium conditions to machine precision, carry the derivatives of the vapour fraction and of the phase compositions with respect to $P$ and $T$ by the implicit function theorem, so that the tangents are those of the converged solution and not of a truncated iteration. Functions with restricted domains (square roots, logarithms, powers) are guarded before evaluation so that the tangents stay finite, and the flash runs inside the compiled program, since an eager call would be retraced at every step. Third, the compiled march is vectorised over cases with the pipe and inlet parameters as arguments: one compilation serves any number of cases with the same fluid and grid, and a batch of 300 cases costs roughly the time of the slowest case rather than the sum. This vectorised march is the data generator of Section~\ref{sec:surrogate} and the residual provider of Section~\ref{sec:pinn}.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:implementation}

Each case is a self-contained JSON document (fluid with component properties, pipe profile, inlet conditions, model and solver options) and each recorded run stores the profiles together with the case and the software provenance. The reference curves used below were digitised from the figures of \citet{carvalho2022} and its tabulated appendices were extracted from the document; every figure and table of this article is produced by one script from the recorded runs, the trained networks and these reference data.

\subsection{Offshore production system}\label{sec:offshore}

Fig.~\ref{fig:offshore} and Table~\ref{tab:offshore_points} show the profiles along the well, flowline and riser. The no-slip model reproduces the no-slip curves of the reference: \SI{119.98}{\bar} against \SI{119.97}{\bar} at the wellhead, within \SI{1.7}{\bar} at the riser base, within \SI{1.4}{\kelvin} in temperature everywhere, and annular flow along the whole system, as reported. \subsection{Gas-condensate pipeline}\label{sec:condensate}

The \SI{8}{\kilo\metre} pipeline with a rich gas (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}) was simulated for two inlet temperatures and three heat-transfer coefficients. With the printed values of $U$ the present model cooled the gas far more slowly than the reference figures. Fitting the exponential cooling law $T - T_\mathrm{amb} \propto \exp(-a x)$ to the single-phase part of the six reference profiles gave pure exponentials (residual below \SI{0.1}{\kelvin}) whose decay rate per unit $U$ was the same in all six cases and $3.4\times$ the value of $U\pi D/(\mdot c_p)$ with the printed data, with no additive term and no dependence on temperature. A single constant factor on the heat-loss term therefore explains the whole set, whereas an error in the thermodynamics or in the energy equation is ruled out both by the shape of the profiles and by the agreement in the offshore system; the printed $U$ values are taken as a presentation error of the reference and the effective values $3.42\,U$ are used. Fig.~\ref{fig:condensate} and Table~\ref{tab:condensate} then show agreement of the temperature profiles to \SIrange{0.1}{0.3}{\kelvin}, of the outlet pressure to \SI{0.04}{\bar} and of the onset of condensation to within \SI{1.1}{\kilo\metre}; the dew-point temperature at the onset agrees to \SI{1.5}{\kelvin}. The reference vapour-fraction curves drop discontinuously at the dew point, which the equilibrium flash cannot produce, and then flatten above the present curves: this is the signature of a dead band in the phase-stability test of the reference. The vapour fraction plotted there is molar, which the present curves match to 0.004 in the two-phase region.

\section{Neural surrogates of the physical model}\label{sec:surrogate}

\subsection{Data generation}\label{sec:data}

Following the reference, each segment of the offshore system (well, flowline, riser) was treated as a separate unit with the 11-component fluid of its thermodynamic validation, the no-slip model, inputs $(P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}, \mdot)$ and outputs $(P_\mathrm{out}, T_\mathrm{out})$. Three hundred input triples per segment were drawn by Latin hypercube sampling \citep{mckay1979} within the ranges of the reference (well: \SIrange{145}{185}{\bar}, \SIrange{60}{100}{\celsius}; flowline: \SIrange{60}{100}{\bar}, \SIrange{50}{90}{\celsius}; riser: \SIrange{40}{80}{\bar}, \SIrange{30}{70}{\celsius}; \SIrange{2}{25}{\kilogram\per\second} throughout) and simulated in one vectorised call per segment: 300 converged profiles in \SIrange{67}{104}{\second} including compilation. The reference published its 299 samples per segment with the outputs of its own physical model; simulating the same inputs (Table~\ref{tab:appendix_b}) reproduces its outlet pressure and temperature in the well ($R^2$ of 0.98 and 1.00, mean deviations of \SI{1.4}{\bar} on an \SI{84}{\bar} drop and \SI{0.1}{\kelvin}) and in the riser, which is a direct check of the physical model on 598 independent cases. In the flowline the reference cools $1.4\times$ more than the present model (\SI{34}{\kelvin} against \SI{24}{\kelvin} on average) while losing \SI{19}{\percent} less pressure; as in Section~\ref{sec:condensate}, the thermal discrepancy is confined to a horizontal flowline, and it is reported as open.

\subsection{Networks, training and hyperparameter search}\label{sec:training}

The surrogate of each segment is a multilayer perceptron with fully connected layers of equal width and a linear output, inputs and outputs standardised with the training statistics, weights initialised uniformly in $\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$ and trained with Adam \citep{kingma2015} on the mean squared error in full batch, 70\,\% of the samples for training and 30\,\% for testing. The training loop runs inside the same compiled framework as the simulator, with the epochs unrolled by a scan, so that a network trains in seconds. The hyperparameter grid of the reference, one to three hidden layers, sigmoid, hyperbolic-tangent or rectified-linear activation, 5 to 25 neurons and learning rates of 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001, 135 networks per segment, was trained in full with the same data split and 2000 epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}, Table~\ref{tab:grid}). With these data 131 to 133 of the 135 networks reach $R^2 > 0.9$ on both outputs, against 8 to 35 in the reference, where many configurations diverged at the largest learning rate or did not converge at the smallest; the difference is explained by the amount of optimisation rather than by the data, since the reference's training curves stop at about 200 iterations. The configuration adopted by the reference, one hidden layer of 20 rectified-linear units and a learning rate of 0.01, is kept for the final models for comparability; it is within $10^{-3}$ in $R^2$ of the best network of the grid. Fig.~\ref{fig:training} shows the training and test errors and Fig.~\ref{fig:parity} the test parity; Table~\ref{tab:test} gives $R^2$ of 0.9996--0.9999 for pressure and 0.997--0.999 for temperature, with mean errors below \SI{0.4}{\percent}, against 0.96--0.98 and 0.91--0.94 in the reference.

\subsection{Chained prediction and performance gain}\label{sec:chain}

The three surrogates chained in series, the outlet of one segment feeding the next with the same mass flow rate, replace the physical model of the whole system (Fig.~\ref{fig:chain}, Table~\ref{tab:chain}). For the inlet of the reference figures (\SI{170}{\bar}, \SI{80}{\celsius}, \SI{20}{\kilogram\per\second}) the chain stays within \SI{0.25}{\percent} in pressure and \SI{0.1}{\percent} in temperature of the physical model, against \SIrange{1}{4.7}{\percent} and \SIrange{0.3}{0.6}{\percent} in the reference. The reference physical profile in Fig.~\ref{fig:chain} ends \SI{8}{\kelvin} colder than the present one, which is the flowline cooling difference of Table~\ref{tab:appendix_b}. The surrogate, however, is only as good as its training domain and carries no notion of the conservation laws, which motivates the extension of the next section.

\section{Physics-informed surrogates of the profiles}\label{sec:pinn}

\subsection{The axial position as a feature}\label{sec:profile}

A surrogate that returns only the outlet of a segment cannot locate the dew point, the point where a pattern changes or the temperature at a given depth. Adding the dimensionless position $s = x/L$ to the inputs, $(P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}, \mdot, s)$, lets the network return the whole profile, and the known inlet condition can be imposed exactly instead of learned \citep{lagaris1998}: the network $\mathbf{g}_{\btheta}$ predicts the drop per unit position and the profile is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile}
\bu_{\btheta}(s) = \bu_\mathrm{in} - s\,\mathbf{g}_{\btheta}(P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}, \mdot, s),
\end{equation}
so that $\bu_{\btheta}(0) = \bu_\mathrm{in}$ for any $\btheta$. The networks (two hidden layers of 32 hyperbolic-tangent units, 1282 parameters, Adam at 0.01 for 3000 epochs) were trained on the 300 profiles per segment recorded at 51 axial points, 210 profiles for training and 90 for testing, with the targets standardised. Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} and the first rows of Table~\ref{tab:pinn} show that the profile is recovered along the whole segment with root-mean-square errors of \SIrange{0.02}{0.11}{\bar} and \SIrange{0.08}{0.24}{\kelvin}, of the same order as the outlet surrogates of Section~\ref{sec:surrogate}.

\subsection{A residual with physical meaning}\label{sec:residual}

A physics-informed network \citep{raissi2019,karniadakis2021} adds to the data loss the residual of the governing equations at collocation points that need not be simulated. For the present problem the residual must mean conservation of momentum and energy with the same thermodynamics and closures as the simulator, which the flux form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} provides directly: with $\mathbf{f}(x, \bu; \mdot) = \bJ^{-1}\bS$ the right-hand side of the marching form, Eq.~\eqref{eq:march}, the scaled residual of the network profile at a collocation point $(P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}, \mdot, s)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual}
\br_{\btheta} = \frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\left[\frac{1}{L}\frac{\partial\bu_{\btheta}}{\partial s} - \mathbf{f}\!\left(sL, \bu_{\btheta}(s); \mdot\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\partial\bu_{\btheta}/\partial s$ is the exact derivative of the network with respect to its position input, obtained by a forward-mode tangent, $\mathbf{f}$ is evaluated by the simulator's own compiled right-hand side (flash, closures and the exact $\bJ$ included), and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the median slope $|\dd\bu/\dd x|$ of the recorded profiles, so that the two components, in \si{\pascal\per\metre} and \si{\kelvin\per\metre}, are of order one when the error is of the order of the typical gradient. The residual thus vanishes exactly when the network profile solves the flow equations for that inlet condition and flow rate; it is not a penalty on a simplified or linearised model but on the full compositional model. Collocation points are drawn by Latin hypercube sampling in the box of the inputs times $s \in (0, 1]$, and the loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L}(\btheta) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{\text{data}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\btheta} - \mathbf{y}\right\|^2}_{\text{simulated profiles}} + \lambda\,\underbrace{\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{\text{collocation}}\left\|\br_{\btheta}\right\|^2}_{\text{governing equations}},
\end{equation}
with the data term on the standardised targets of Eq.~\eqref{eq:profile}.

Differentiating the residual term with respect to $\btheta$ raises a difficulty specific to compositional models: $\mathbf{f}$ contains the flash, an iterative loop, through which reverse-mode differentiation is not available, and a second level of differentiation on top of the linearisation that produces $\bJ$ exhausts memory at compile time. The gradient is obtained instead without differentiating through $\mathbf{f}$ at all. At each step the network values $\bu_{\btheta}$ and $\partial\bu_{\btheta}/\partial s$ are evaluated at the collocation points, then $\mathbf{f}$ and its Jacobian $\partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\bu$ are evaluated there as plain numbers (the Jacobian by forward differences with the steps \SI{1}{\kilo\pascal} and \SI{0.01}{\kelvin}, as in the reference), and the residual $\br_0$ and the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_0$ are detached from the graph. The auxiliary scalar
\begin{equation}\label{eq:surrogate_loss}
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\btheta) = \frac{2}{N_c}\sum_{\text{collocation}} \frac{\br_0}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{L}\frac{\partial\bu_{\btheta}}{\partial s} - \mathbf{J}_0\,\bu_{\btheta}\right]
\end{equation}
has, at the current parameters, exactly the gradient of the residual term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}, by the chain rule, and involves only the network; its reverse-mode gradient is cheap. Each epoch therefore costs three right-hand-side evaluations per collocation point, about \SI{20}{\milli\second} per point on a laptop CPU, and the step compiles in a few minutes.

\subsection{Sparse-data study}\label{sec:pinn_results}

To test whether the residual term supplies what missing data cannot, the profile networks were retrained with only 20 of the 210 training profiles, with and without the residual term ($N_c = 60$ collocation points, $\lambda = 1$, 300 epochs), starting either from the weights of the data-only network or from a random initialisation, and evaluated on the same 90 test profiles and on the residual at the collocation set (Table~\ref{tab:pinn}). With 20 profiles the data-only network loses one order of magnitude with respect to the full data set: root-mean-square errors of \SI{0.18}{\bar} and \SI{1.3}{\kelvin} in the well, \SI{0.83}{\bar} and \SI{3.1}{\kelvin} in the flowline and \SI{0.11}{\bar} and \SI{0.22}{\kelvin} in the riser, with scaled residuals of 0.08--0.37, that is, the fitted profiles violate the balances by up to a third of the typical gradient. Adding the residual term with the same 20 profiles brings the errors to \SI{0.15}{\bar} and \SI{0.61}{\kelvin}, \SI{0.17}{\bar} and \SI{1.1}{\kelvin}, and \SI{0.08}{\bar} and \SI{0.16}{\kelvin}: the temperature error is halved in the well and divided by three in the flowline, the flowline pressure error by five, and the residual drops to 0.006--0.010, below that of the network trained on 210 profiles (0.005--0.046), which was never asked to satisfy the equations. Training from a random initialisation with the full loss reaches similar errors with a residual of 0.008--0.046. The gain is largest where the data-only network is worst, the flowline, whose temperature drop spans \SI{40}{\kelvin}; the remaining error is concentrated in the two-phase part of the profiles, where the gradients change fastest. Fig.~\ref{fig:pinn} shows the error of the three networks along the whole system, each segment fed with the physical inlet of that segment.

\subsection{Overfitting, bias and the choice of the test set}\label{sec:validation}

Synthetic data are cheap here, which invites training on ever larger sets without asking whether the network generalises or merely interpolates its training points. Three measures are reported (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}, Tables~\ref{tab:validation} and \ref{tab:residual}). First, learning curves: the test and training errors as functions of the number of training profiles, over repeated random splits, where the test--train gap measures overfitting and the spread between repetitions the variance of the estimate; a five-fold cross-validation of the outlet surrogate gives the same quantities on disjoint test folds. Second, the bias, the mean signed error on the test set, which a symmetric error measure hides. Third, three ways of choosing the test set for the profile surrogate, because the training rows are not independent samples: the 50 axial points of one profile share the same inlet condition. Splitting by profile keeps whole profiles out of training and tests generalisation to new inlet conditions, which is the use case; splitting by axial position keeps all profiles but hides \SI{30}{\percent} of the axial points, which tests interpolation along $x$ only and is the split a careless row-wise shuffle would effectively produce; a block split holds out the profiles with the highest \SI{30}{\percent} of inlet pressures and tests extrapolation. For the physics-informed network the residual of the governing equations is evaluated both on the collocation set used in training and on a second set never seen, which is a test that needs no simulated data at all.

The learning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}) show the expected regime change. With ten training samples the outlet surrogate interpolates them exactly (training error zero) while the test error is \SIrange{1}{2.7}{\bar} and \SIrange{1.7}{4}{\kelvin}, pure overfitting; the test error then falls steadily with the number of samples and the gap to the training error closes to \SIrange{0.04}{0.3}{\bar} at 210 samples. Five-fold cross-validation puts the outlet test error at $0.14 \pm 0.05$, $0.55 \pm 0.23$ and $0.07 \pm 0.02$\,bar and $0.38 \pm 0.21$, $0.42 \pm 0.07$ and $0.21 \pm 0.10$\,K for the well, flowline and riser, with biases below \SI{0.07}{\bar} and \SI{0.02}{\kelvin}: the spread between folds is \SIrange{30}{50}{\percent} of the mean, so a single split, as in Table~\ref{tab:test}, overstates the precision of the error estimate even when it does not bias it. For the profile surrogate the three test-set strategies give very different answers (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). Splitting by axial position reports errors of a few hundredths of a bar and a few hundredths of a kelvin even with ten training profiles, equal to the training error: interpolation along $x$ within known profiles is trivial for a network that takes $s$ as an input, and a row-wise shuffle would report this number as the generalisation error, \num{10} to \num{50} times too small. Splitting by profile gives the honest figure, \SI{0.19}{\bar} and \SI{1.2}{\kelvin} in the well with 20 profiles and \SI{0.03}{\bar} and \SI{0.15}{\kelvin} with 210, consistent with Section~\ref{sec:pinn_results}. The block split, which asks the network to extrapolate to inlet pressures above its training range, costs a further factor of two to five in the pressure error of the riser and exposes a systematic bias ($+0.22$\,bar with 20 profiles, $+0.04$\,bar with 210) that the random splits hide; temperature, which depends weakly on the inlet pressure, is almost unaffected. Finally, the residual of the governing equations on a collocation set never used (Table~\ref{tab:residual}) is \numrange{0.03}{0.12} for the physics-informed networks against \numrange{0.006}{0.010} on their own collocation set: the network satisfies the equations where it was asked to, and partially elsewhere, which is the counterpart of overfitting for the physics term; the remedy is cheap, since collocation points cost no simulation, and even so the held-out residual of the physics-informed networks stays two to four times below that of the data-only networks trained on the same 20 profiles.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Writing the compositional two-phase pipe-flow model in flux form on an array backend with automatic differentiation gave exact Jacobians and flash derivatives, three solvers that verify one another to \SI{0.01}{\bar} on the same equations, global balances that close to machine precision in the finite-volume scheme, Newton convergence in one to three iterations across phase and pattern changes, and a vectorised march that generates hundreds of converged profiles per minute. Against the reference implementation the no-slip model reproduces the offshore system and, with an effective heat-transfer coefficient identified from the shape of the reference profiles, the gas-condensate pipeline; the mechanistic model reproduces the vertical pressure drops and leaves the horizontal ones, lower than the reference by about one half, as an open question for which the unprinted closure details of the reference are the suspect. The vectorised simulator made the surrogate workflow of the reference reproducible end to end, with the full hyperparameter grid trained in minutes and final networks that reproduce the physical model to $R^2 > 0.997$ and evaluate in microseconds, and it provided the residual of the governing equations for a physics-informed surrogate that returns the whole profile with the inlet condition built in. The construction of that residual, the simulator's own right-hand side at collocation points, scaled by the typical gradients and differentiated without back-propagating through the flash, is general for any model with an iterative thermodynamic kernel. With one tenth of the training profiles, the residual term halved to quartered the test errors of the profile surrogate and brought its violation of the conservation equations below that of the network trained on all the data, so that the physics term buys accuracy that the data alone would require many more simulations to deliver.
