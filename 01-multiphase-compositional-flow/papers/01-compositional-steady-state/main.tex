\begin{abstract}
% <= 250 palavras, autocontido, sem referências (texto literal: a classe CAS grava o resumo num ficheiro auxiliar).
Steady-state compositional simulation of gas--liquid flow in wells, flowlines and risers couples a cubic equation of state and an isothermal flash to mixture momentum and energy balances closed by homogeneous or flow-pattern-dependent mechanistic relations. This article, the first of two, presents a simulator in which the physics is written once, in flux form, and executed on an array backend with automatic differentiation (JAX): the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes, the derivatives of the flash with respect to pressure and temperature and the sparse Newton Jacobians are exact, and the compiled program is vectorised over cases. A no-slip and a mechanistic model are solved by an adaptive Runge--Kutta march and by finite-difference and finite-volume nodal systems; the three methods agree to 0.01 bar and 0.1 K, the finite volumes close the momentum and energy balances to machine precision, and Newton converges in one to three iterations across phase and flow-pattern changes. The thermodynamic kernel reproduces an independent open-source Peng--Robinson implementation to a relative 1e-6. Compared with Aspen HYSYS, the phase envelope of an 11-component fluid agrees to 0.7 bar in the median; in 100 vertical lines the mechanistic model falls inside the range of four HYSYS correlations in 88 % of the cases, with a median deviation of 1 % from their mean, while in 100 short horizontal lines with a heavy pseudo-component both models lie below that range, which itself spans a factor of three; along an offshore well--flowline--riser system the mechanistic pressure follows the Beggs and Brill profile of HYSYS within about 1 bar after the well and the temperatures agree within 2 K. Once compiled, the nodal solvers run the offshore system in about 0.6 s.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Flow assurance in offshore production depends on steady-state models of gas--liquid flow along wells, flowlines and risers that predict pressure, temperature, phase fractions and flow pattern from the fluid composition and the boundary conditions. Commercial simulators such as Aspen HYSYS \citep{aspenhysys} offer a choice of empirical and mechanistic correlations for this purpose \citep{beggs1973,aziz1972,mandhane1974,zhang2003}, and the mechanistic, flow-pattern-based closures of \citet{taitel1976,taitel1980}, \citet{xiao1990} and \citet{gomez2000} remain the basis of most pipeline models. Compositional formulations couple them to a cubic equation of state \citep{peng1976} and an isothermal flash \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b} so that phase behaviour, condensation and heat transfer are resolved along the pipe; the formulation used here, with a homogeneous no-slip model and a mechanistic model with slip, was developed in the first author's master's thesis \citep{carvalho2022}.

Two difficulties recur in this class of simulators. The first is numerical: the derivatives that drive both a marching solution (the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes with respect to pressure and temperature, which contains the derivatives of the flash) and the Newton iterations of a nodal solution are usually approximated by finite differences, each of which costs additional flash calculations and introduces noise that interferes with convergence where phases appear or disappear. The second is cost: the flash dominates the run time, and the thousands of evaluations needed for optimisation, uncertainty quantification or surrogate training multiply it.

This article presents a reimplementation of the compositional model designed around automatic differentiation and vectorisation \citep{jax2018}. The contributions are: (i) a flux-form statement of both flow models on a single interface, with exact Jacobians and flash derivatives obtained by forward-mode automatic differentiation; (ii) three interchangeable solvers (adaptive march, finite differences, finite volumes) that verify each other on the same equations, with global balance diagnostics, and a verification of the thermodynamic kernel against an independent open-source implementation; (iii) a comparison with Aspen HYSYS on the phase envelope and properties of an 11-component fluid, on 200 horizontal and vertical pressure-drop lines and on an offshore well--flowline--riser system; and (iv) a vectorised march that generates hundreds of converged profiles per minute. The companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b} uses this simulator as the data generator and as the residual provider of neural and physics-informed surrogate models.

Section~\ref{sec:model} states the model, Section~\ref{sec:numerics} the numerical methods and the differentiable implementation, Section~\ref{sec:verification} the verification, Section~\ref{sec:hysys} the comparison with HYSYS, Section~\ref{sec:application} an application to a gas-condensate pipeline and Section~\ref{sec:performance} the computational performance.

\section{Mathematical model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Conservation equations in flux form}\label{sec:conservation}

A single pipe of length $L$ is described by segments of constant diameter $D$, roughness $\varepsilon$, inclination $\theta$, overall heat-transfer coefficient $U$ and ambient temperature $T_\mathrm{amb}$. The total mass flow rate $\mdot$ is constant; the vapour and liquid rates follow from the flash, $\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$ and $\mdot_L = (1 - w_G)\mdot$. Both flow models considered here are written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flux}
\frac{\dd \bPhi}{\dd x} = \bS, \qquad
\bPhi = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\mdot_G v_G + \mdot_L v_L}{A} + P \\[1.2ex] \dfrac{\mdot_G h_G + \mdot_L h_L}{\mdot} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\bS = \begin{pmatrix} -F - \rho_m g \sin\theta \\[0.6ex] \dfrac{Q_w}{\mdot} - g\sin\theta \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the first component is the mixture momentum balance (momentum flux plus pressure, with wall friction $F$ and the hydrostatic term with the in situ mixture density $\rho_m = \rho_G(1 - H_L) + \rho_L H_L$) and the second is the mixture energy balance in terms of the mass-weighted specific enthalpy, with the potential-energy term and the heat exchanged with the surroundings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qw}
Q_w = -\pi D\, U\, (T - T_\mathrm{amb}).
\end{equation}
The kinetic-energy contribution to the energy balance is neglected; it is available as an option. The state vector is $\bu = (P, T)$: for a given composition and $\mdot$, every quantity in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:flux}--\eqref{eq:qw} is a local function of $\bu$ through the flash and the closure relations. The two models differ only in how the phase velocities and the friction term are closed.

\subsection{Thermodynamic model}\label{sec:thermo}

Phase equilibrium and phase properties come from the Peng--Robinson equation of state \citep{peng1976},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr}
P = \frac{RT}{v - b} - \frac{a\,\alpha(T)}{v(v + b) + b(v - b)},
\end{equation}
with the classical mixing rules and zero binary interaction parameters (Appendix~\ref{app:eos}). The isothermal flash solves the Rachford--Rice equation \citep{rachford1952} with successive substitution of the equilibrium ratios followed by Newton polishing \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b}, preceded by a stability test. Phase densities follow from the compressibility roots, enthalpies from the ideal-gas heat-capacity polynomials, the enthalpies of formation and the residual enthalpy of the equation of state, liquid viscosity from the corresponding-states correlation of \citet{allan1991}, vapour viscosity from \citet{pedersen1987} and surface tension from the parachor method. The component data are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:data}. The no-slip liquid volume fraction follows directly from the flash,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda}
\lambda_L = \frac{\mdot_L/\rho_L}{\mdot_L/\rho_L + \mdot_G/\rho_G}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closure relations}\label{sec:closures}

In the no-slip model the two phases share the velocity $v = \mdot/(\rho_m A)$ with $\rho_m = \rho_G(1-\lambda_L) + \rho_L\lambda_L$, and the friction term uses the Churchill friction factor \citep{churchill1977} with volume-weighted mixture density and viscosity. In the mechanistic model the flow pattern is identified by the Taitel--Dukler criteria \citep{taitel1976,taitel1980}, and the holdup and the friction term follow from the pattern-specific momentum balances of \citet{xiao1990} for stratified and annular flow and of \citet{gomez2000} for slug flow, with bubble flow treated by a drift-flux relation; the in situ holdup
\begin{equation}\label{eq:holdup}
H_L = \frac{S\lambda_L}{1 - \lambda_L + S\lambda_L}
\end{equation}
is expressed through the slip ratio $S = v_G/v_L$. The pattern map and the transitions are evaluated at every state, so that the pattern may change along the pipe.

\section{Numerical methods and differentiable implementation}\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Three solvers on one interface}\label{sec:solvers}

Because both models expose the same pair $(\bPhi, \bS)$ as functions of $\bu$, the solution methods are written once and apply to both. Three are used.

\paragraph{Marching} With $\bJ = \partial\bPhi/\partial\bu$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} becomes the initial-value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:march}
\bJ(\bu)\,\frac{\dd\bu}{\dd x} = \bS(\bu), \qquad \bu(0) = (P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}),
\end{equation}
integrated with the embedded Dormand--Prince 5(4) pair \citep{dormand1980} with adaptive step, from output point to output point, with the error measured on the state scaled by (\SI{1}{\bar}, \SI{1}{\kelvin}). Expanding $\dd\bPhi/\dd x$ term by term and evaluating the partial derivatives of the flash outputs by one-sided finite differences costs three flashes per evaluation; here $\bJ$ is exact (Section~\ref{sec:ad}) and a single flash is needed per right-hand-side evaluation. The converged equilibrium ratios of the last accepted step are carried as an auxiliary variable and seed the next flash, which halves the substitution iterations without changing the result.

\paragraph{Finite differences} With $P$ and $T$ as unknowns at $n$ nodes per segment, the flux derivative is discretised with the second-order operator $\mathbf{M}$ (centred inside, one-sided at the ends), $(\mathbf{M}\bPhi)_i = \bS_i$. For the mechanistic model the phase mass rates are eliminated algebraically ($\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$), which keeps two unknowns per node.

\paragraph{Finite volumes} Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} over each cell gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fv}
\bPhi_{i+1} - \bPhi_i = \frac{h}{2}\left(\bS_i + \bS_{i+1}\right),
\end{equation}
which is conservative by construction and never differentiates the fluxes across the kinks introduced by the dew point or by a change of flow pattern. Break points between segments are duplicated with continuity of $P$ and $T$. Both nodal systems are solved by Newton's method,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newton}
\bJ_N(\bu^{k})\,\delta\bu = -\mathbf{R}(\bu^{k}), \qquad \bu^{k+1} = \bu^{k} + \delta\bu,
\end{equation}
with the exact banded Jacobian assembled by forward-mode automatic differentiation with graph colouring (bandwidth two for the finite-difference operator, one for the finite volumes), starting from the no-slip march interpolated at the nodes. Every run reports the global balances $(\bPhi_\mathrm{out} - \bPhi_\mathrm{in}) - \int \bS\,\dd x$ per segment, evaluated on the solver's own grid, as a diagnostic.

\subsection{Automatic differentiation and vectorisation}\label{sec:ad}

The physics is written once against an injected array backend; the same code runs in NumPy, for inspection and small cases, and in JAX \citep{jax2018}, where it is compiled just in time and transformed. Three transformations matter here. First, forward-mode linearisation gives $\bJ$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:march} and the coloured Newton Jacobian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:newton} exactly, at the cost of two tangent evaluations instead of three extra flashes. Second, the flash is itself differentiable: the successive-substitution iterations are excluded from the derivative graph and the Newton polishing steps, which solve the equilibrium conditions to machine precision, carry the derivatives of the vapour fraction and of the phase compositions with respect to $P$ and $T$ by the implicit function theorem, so that the tangents are those of the converged solution and not of a truncated iteration. Functions with restricted domains (square roots, logarithms, powers) are guarded before evaluation so that the tangents stay finite, and the flash runs inside the compiled program, since an eager call would be retraced at every step. Third, the compiled march is vectorised over cases with the pipe and inlet parameters as arguments, so that one compilation serves any number of cases with the same fluid and grid and a whole batch is evaluated in a single call. This vectorised march is the data generator of the companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b}, where the same compiled right-hand side also provides the residual of the governing equations for physics-informed surrogates.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:implementation}

Each case is a self-contained JSON document (fluid with component properties, pipe profile, inlet conditions, model and solver options) and each recorded run stores the profiles together with the case and the software provenance. Every figure and table of this article is produced by one script from the recorded runs and the reference data.

\section{Comparison with Aspen HYSYS}\label{sec:hysys}

The HYSYS results used here were computed by the first author with Aspen HYSYS \citep{aspenhysys}, Peng--Robinson property package and pipe segments with the correlations listed in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, for the same fluids and conditions \citep{carvalho2022}. The C$_{7+}$ pseudo-component of the pressure-drop and offshore fluids was characterised in HYSYS and its properties are listed in Table~\ref{tab:components}. The pressure drops of the 200 lines are tabulated values; the phase envelope, the property distributions and the offshore profiles are available as plots and were digitised. All of them are provided as supplementary material.

\subsection{Phase envelope and fluid properties}\label{sec:envelope}

Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope} compares the phase envelope of the 11-component fluid computed by the present flash with the HYSYS envelope: the bubble line agrees with a median deviation of \SI{0.66}{\bar} and the dew line of \SI{0.69}{\bar} at the same temperature, with the largest deviations (up to \SI{11}{\bar} on the bubble line) near the critical point, where both lines are steep and the digitisation is least precise. The HYSYS values of the phase properties at the 159 states inside the envelope are available only as the markers of parity plots, without the state each marker belongs to, so the comparison is of distributions: Fig.~\ref{fig:properties} plots the quantiles of the present values against those of HYSYS, and Table~\ref{tab:properties} gives the ratios of the medians and of the 10th and 90th percentiles. Overlapping markers merge in the digitisation, which biases the digitised distribution towards rare values, so the present 159 values are merged in the same way before the quantiles are computed. Liquid density, liquid enthalpy, vapour fraction and surface tension follow the diagonal, with median ratios of 0.97--1.01 except for the vapour fraction (1.15, from the upper tail); the vapour density agrees up to \SI{100}{\kilogram\per\metre\cubed}, with the densest vapour states lower; the vapour enthalpy is offset by up to \SI{0.4}{\mega\joule\per\kilogram} in the middle of its range, which reflects the ideal-gas heat-capacity and formation-enthalpy data of the two packages rather than the equation of state, since the residual part is verified in Section~\ref{sec:thermo_verification}. The vapour viscosity is about \SI{15}{\percent} below HYSYS over the whole range, the difference between the corresponding-states method of \citet{pedersen1987} and the HYSYS method, and the liquid viscosity agrees below \SI{1}{\milli\pascal\second} with a higher tail.

\subsection{Pressure drop in horizontal and vertical lines}\label{sec:dp}

The 200 lines are \SI{100}{\metre} long, adiabatic, horizontal or vertical, with four diameters, five inlet pressures and five mass flow rates (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}), and carry a binary mixture of methane and the C$_{7+}$ pseudo-component (70/30 mol). HYSYS computed each line with four correlations: \citet{beggs1973}, the Tulsa unified model \citep{zhang2003}, the HTFS liquid-slip method, and Gregory--Aziz--Mandhane \citep{mandhane1974} in the horizontal or Aziz--Govier--Fogarasi \citep{aziz1972} in the vertical lines. Fig.~\ref{fig:dp} shows, case by case, the range spanned by the four correlations and the pressure drop of the two present models, and Table~\ref{tab:dp} summarises the deviations.

In the vertical lines, where the hydrostatic term dominates and the four HYSYS correlations agree within \SI{20}{\percent} of each other in the median, the mechanistic model falls inside their range in 88\,\% of the cases, with a median deviation of $+1$\,\% from their geometric mean, $+2$\,\% from Tulsa and $-6$\,\% from Beggs and Brill; the no-slip model, which cannot raise the holdup above the no-slip fraction, is \SI{14}{\percent} lower, as expected, and drops out of the range at low pressure and large diameter, where slip matters most. In the horizontal lines the four HYSYS correlations themselves disagree by a factor of three in the median, and both present models lie below the whole range in 87--95\,\% of the cases: the mechanistic model is \SI{52}{\percent} below Tulsa and \SI{73}{\percent} below Beggs and Brill in the median. These lines are short, adiabatic and friction-dominated, with pressure drops from $10^{-4}$ to \SI{1}{\bar}, and their liquid is the heavy pseudo-component (\SI{560}{\gram\per\mole}), so they are sensitive both to the liquid viscosity and to the stratified-flow closure. The liquid viscosity given by the corresponding-states correlation is \SIrange{0.7}{3}{\milli\pascal\second} at these conditions, decreasing with pressure as methane dissolves in the liquid; tripling it raises the computed pressure drops by about one fifth, so viscosity alone does not explain the gap. Closing it requires the liquid viscosity and holdup that HYSYS computes for the same states, which are not available, and the horizontal comparison is reported as an open discrepancy.

\subsection{Offshore production system}\label{sec:offshore}

The offshore system consists of a vertical well of \SI{1500}{\metre} and \SI{127}{\milli\metre}, a horizontal flowline of \SI{6000}{\metre} and a vertical riser of \SI{300}{\metre}, both of \SI{152.4}{\milli\metre}, with overall heat-transfer coefficients of 7 and \SI{5}{\watt\per\metre\squared\per\kelvin} and ambient temperatures of 40 and \SI{12}{\celsius}; a 9-component fluid with the C$_{7+}$ pseudo-component enters the well at \SI{170}{\bar}, \SI{80}{\celsius} and \SI{10}{\kilogram\per\second}. HYSYS computed the system with the Tulsa and Beggs and Brill correlations. Fig.~\ref{fig:offshore} and Table~\ref{tab:offshore} compare the profiles. The two HYSYS correlations differ by up to \SI{10}{\bar} in pressure, and the present models fall on either side: the no-slip model follows Tulsa in the well and in the first half of the flowline (\SI{120.0}{\bar} against \SI{118.7}{\bar} at the wellhead, \SI{117.1}{\bar} against \SI{117.8}{\bar} at mid-flowline), while the mechanistic model loses more pressure in the well (\SI{115.6}{\bar} at the wellhead, between Tulsa and Beggs and Brill) and then follows the Beggs and Brill profile within about \SI{1}{\bar} along the flowline and at the riser base (\SI{102.3}{\bar} against \SI{102.6}{\bar}). The temperatures agree within \SI{2}{\kelvin} everywhere, the present ones being 0.1 to \SI{1.7}{\kelvin} warmer. The no-slip holdup, the flash fraction $\lambda_L$, is below both HYSYS holdups, as it must be when the gas moves faster than the liquid; the mechanistic holdup lies between the two correlations in the well, below them in the flowline (0.34 against 0.38 and 0.45) and above them at the riser base. The mechanistic model predicts annular flow along the whole system, whereas the Beggs and Brill correlation in HYSYS reports intermittent flow throughout, a difference of classification that the pressure profiles absorb.

\section{Application: condensation in a gas pipeline}\label{sec:application}

An \SI{8}{\kilo\metre} horizontal pipeline of \SI{12}{in} carries a rich gas of methane to $n$-pentane (Table~\ref{tab:fluids}) at \SI{70}{\bar} and \SI{15}{\kilogram\per\second}, with the ambient at \SI{25}{\celsius}. Six cases combine two inlet temperatures (60 and \SI{80}{\celsius}) with three overall heat-transfer coefficients (3.4, 10.3 and \SI{20.5}{\watt\per\metre\squared\per\kelvin}, from an insulated to a bare line), and the question is whether and where condensate forms. Fig.~\ref{fig:condensate} and Table~\ref{tab:condensate} show the results of the no-slip model. With the lowest coefficient the gas reaches the outlet at 43 and \SI{54}{\celsius} and stays single-phase. With the two higher coefficients it reaches the dew point between 3.0 and \SI{7.6}{\kilo\metre}, always at \SIrange{34.0}{34.3}{\celsius}, the dew temperature of the fluid at the local pressure, and leaves with 0.3 to 3.9 mol\% of liquid; the higher inlet temperature only delays the onset. The march resolves the onset without special treatment, since the flash and its derivatives are continuous across the dew point, and the finite-volume solver reproduces the same profiles (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}, case 3).

\section{Computational performance}\label{sec:performance}

Table~\ref{tab:performance} lists wall times on the offshore system. The first run of each model structure includes the just-in-time compilation, \SIrange{13}{38}{\second}, which is paid once and reused for any other inlet condition, pipe parameter or flow rate. Once compiled, the nodal systems with 81 nodes take \SIrange{0.5}{0.7}{\second}, one to three Newton iterations with the exact coloured Jacobian, and the adaptive march takes \SI{1.2}{\second} for the no-slip and \SI{4.1}{\second} for the mechanistic model, with 158 accepted steps and 157 output points. The NumPy backend, which evaluates the same equations without compilation and with finite-difference derivatives, takes \SI{15}{\second} for the finite-volume system, 25 times the compiled JAX run, and \SI{73}{\second} for the mechanistic march. For many cases of one segment the compilation dominates: a march of the well takes \SI{46}{\second} the first time and \SI{0.25}{\second} once compiled, and the vectorised march evaluates 300 cases in \SI{112}{\second} including compilation and in \SI{54}{\second} once compiled, \SI{0.18}{\second} per case. On a CPU, vectorisation thus gains about \SI{30}{\percent} per case over compiled calls made one by one; its main benefit is that a single compilation serves the whole batch, which is how the companion paper generates its training sets in about a minute per segment.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Writing the compositional two-phase pipe-flow model in flux form on an array backend with automatic differentiation gave exact Jacobians and flash derivatives, three solvers that verify one another to \SI{0.01}{\bar} on the same equations, global balances that close to machine precision in the finite-volume scheme, Newton convergence in one to three iterations across phase and pattern changes, and a vectorised march that generates hundreds of converged profiles per minute. The thermodynamic kernel reproduces an independent open-source Peng--Robinson implementation to a relative $10^{-6}$ and the HYSYS phase envelope to \SI{0.7}{\bar} in the median. Against the HYSYS pipe correlations, the mechanistic model falls inside their range in 88\,\% of 100 vertical lines and follows the Beggs and Brill profile of an offshore system within about \SI{1}{\bar} along the flowline, with temperatures within \SI{2}{\kelvin}; in short horizontal lines carrying a heavy pseudo-component both models lie below the range of the HYSYS correlations, which themselves span a factor of three, and this discrepancy, sensitive to the liquid viscosity and to the stratified-flow closure, is left open. The finite-volume scheme on the flux interface is the recommended nodal method, since it is conservative, narrow-banded and insensitive to the kinks of the dew point and of the pattern transitions, while the march remains the tool of choice for generating data. The companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b} builds on both: the vectorised march generates the training sets of neural surrogates of the production system, and the compiled right-hand side supplies the residual of the governing equations to physics-informed surrogates that return whole profiles with the axial position as an input.
