\begin{abstract}
% <= 250 palavras, autocontido, sem referências (texto literal: a classe CAS grava o resumo num ficheiro auxiliar).
Steady-state compositional simulation of gas--liquid flow in wells, flowlines and risers couples a cubic equation of state and an isothermal flash to mixture momentum and energy balances closed by homogeneous or flow-pattern-dependent mechanistic relations. This article, the first of two, presents a simulator in which the physics is written once, in flux form, and executed on an array backend with automatic differentiation (JAX): the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes, the derivatives of the flash outputs with respect to pressure and temperature and the sparse Newton Jacobian of the nodal systems are exact, and the same compiled program is vectorised over many cases. Two flow models, a homogeneous no-slip model and a mechanistic model with slip, are solved by three interchangeable methods, an adaptive Runge--Kutta march and finite-difference and finite-volume nodal systems, and are cross-verified on an offshore well--flowline--riser system and on a gas-condensate pipeline to within 0.01 bar and 0.1 K, with global momentum and energy balances closed to machine precision by the finite-volume scheme and Newton convergence in one to three iterations across phase and flow-pattern changes. The simulator is compared with the reference implementation on 200 pressure-drop cases, the offshore system and the gas-condensate pipeline: it reproduces the reference where the physics is shared, isolates the remaining discrepancies to their origin, and runs the offshore system in a fraction of a second once compiled, with a vectorised march that produces hundreds of converged profiles per minute. The second part of the work uses this simulator as the data generator and as the residual provider of neural and physics-informed surrogate models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Flow assurance in offshore production depends on steady-state models of gas--liquid flow along wells, flowlines and risers that predict pressure, temperature, phase fractions and flow pattern from the fluid composition and the boundary conditions. The mechanistic, flow-pattern-based closures of \citet{taitel1976,taitel1980}, \citet{xiao1990} and \citet{gomez2000} remain the workhorse of pipeline design, and compositional formulations couple them to a cubic equation of state \citep{peng1976} and an isothermal flash \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b} so that phase behaviour, condensation and heat transfer are resolved along the pipe. \citet{carvalho2022} developed such a formulation with two flow models, a homogeneous no-slip model marched as an initial-value problem and a mechanistic model with slip solved as a nodal nonlinear system, validated it against a commercial simulator on 200 pressure-drop cases and two production systems, and used it to train neural surrogate models.

Two difficulties recur in this class of simulators. The first is numerical: the derivatives that drive both the march (the Jacobian of the conserved fluxes with respect to pressure and temperature, which contains the derivatives of the flash) and the Newton iterations of the nodal systems are usually approximated by finite differences, each of which costs additional flash calculations and introduces noise that interferes with convergence across phase changes; the reference implementation reported fragile convergence of its root finder where phases appear or disappear. The second is cost: the flash dominates the run time, and the thousands of evaluations needed for optimisation, uncertainty quantification or surrogate training multiply it.

This article reports a reimplementation of the model of \citet{carvalho2022} designed around automatic differentiation and vectorisation \citep{jax2018}. The contributions are: (i) a flux-form statement of both flow models on a single interface, with exact Jacobians and flash derivatives obtained by forward-mode automatic differentiation; (ii) three interchangeable solvers (adaptive march, finite differences, finite volumes) that verify each other on the same equations, with global balance diagnostics; (iii) a comparison with the reference implementation on three benchmark problems, which reproduces the reference where the physics is shared and isolates the discrepancies to their origin; and (iv) a vectorised march that generates hundreds of converged profiles per minute. The companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b} uses this simulator as the data generator and as the residual provider of neural and physics-informed surrogate models.

Section~\ref{sec:model} states the model, Section~\ref{sec:numerics} the numerical methods and the differentiable implementation, and Section~\ref{sec:results} the verification, validation and performance.

\section{Mathematical model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Conservation equations in flux form}\label{sec:conservation}

A single pipe of length $L$ is described by segments of constant diameter $D$, roughness $\varepsilon$, inclination $\theta$, overall heat-transfer coefficient $U$ and ambient temperature $T_\mathrm{amb}$. The total mass flow rate $\mdot$ is constant; the vapour and liquid rates follow from the flash, $\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$ and $\mdot_L = (1 - w_G)\mdot$. Both flow models considered here are written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flux}
\frac{\dd \bPhi}{\dd x} = \bS, \qquad
\bPhi = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\mdot_G v_G + \mdot_L v_L}{A} + P \\[1.2ex] \dfrac{\mdot_G h_G + \mdot_L h_L}{\mdot} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\bS = \begin{pmatrix} -F - \rho_m g \sin\theta \\[0.6ex] \dfrac{Q_w}{\mdot} - g\sin\theta \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the first component is the mixture momentum balance (momentum flux plus pressure, with wall friction $F$ and the hydrostatic term with the in situ mixture density $\rho_m = \rho_G\alpha_G + \rho_L H_L$) and the second is the mixture energy balance in terms of the mass-weighted specific enthalpy, with the potential-energy term and the heat exchanged with the surroundings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qw}
Q_w = -\pi D\, U\, (T - T_\mathrm{amb}).
\end{equation}
The kinetic-energy contribution to the energy balance is neglected, as in the reference model; it is available as an option. The state vector is $\bu = (P, T)$: for a given composition and $\mdot$, every quantity in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:flux}--\eqref{eq:qw} is a local function of $\bu$ through the flash and the closure relations. The two models differ only in how the phase velocities and the friction term are closed.

\subsection{Thermodynamic model}\label{sec:thermo}

Phase equilibrium and phase properties come from the Peng--Robinson equation of state \citep{peng1976},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr}
P = \frac{RT}{v - b} - \frac{a\,\alpha(T)}{v(v + b) + b(v - b)},
\end{equation}
with the classical mixing rules and zero binary interaction parameters, as in the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:eos}). The isothermal flash solves the Rachford--Rice equation \citep{rachford1952} with successive substitution of the equilibrium ratios followed by Newton polishing \citep{michelsen1982a,michelsen1982b}, preceded by a stability test. Phase densities follow from the compressibility roots, enthalpies from the ideal-gas heat-capacity polynomials and the residual enthalpy of the equation of state, liquid viscosity from the corresponding-states correlation of \citet{allan1991}, vapour viscosity from \citet{pedersen1987} and surface tension from the parachor method; the parameters are those tabulated by \citet{carvalho2022}. The no-slip liquid volume fraction follows directly from the flash,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda}
\lambda_L = \frac{\mdot_L/\rho_L}{\mdot_L/\rho_L + \mdot_G/\rho_G}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Closure relations}\label{sec:closures}

In the no-slip model the two phases share the velocity $v = \mdot/(\rho_m A)$ with $\rho_m = \rho_G(1-\lambda_L) + \rho_L\lambda_L$, and the friction term uses the Churchill friction factor \citep{churchill1977} with volume-weighted mixture density and viscosity. In the mechanistic model the flow pattern is identified by the Taitel--Dukler criteria \citep{taitel1976,taitel1980}, and the holdup and the friction term follow from the pattern-specific momentum balances of \citet{xiao1990} for stratified and annular flow and of \citet{gomez2000} for slug flow, with bubble flow treated by a drift-flux relation; the in situ holdup
\begin{equation}\label{eq:holdup}
H_L = \frac{S\lambda_L}{1 - \lambda_L + S\lambda_L}
\end{equation}
is expressed through the slip ratio $S = v_G/v_L$. The pattern map and the transitions are evaluated at every state, so that the pattern may change along the pipe.

\section{Numerical methods and differentiable implementation}\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Three solvers on one interface}\label{sec:solvers}

Because both models expose the same pair $(\bPhi, \bS)$ as functions of $\bu$, the solution methods are written once and apply to both. Three are used.

\paragraph{Marching} With $\bJ = \partial\bPhi/\partial\bu$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} becomes the initial-value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:march}
\bJ(\bu)\,\frac{\dd\bu}{\dd x} = \bS(\bu), \qquad \bu(0) = (P_\mathrm{in}, T_\mathrm{in}),
\end{equation}
integrated with the embedded Dormand--Prince 5(4) pair \citep{dormand1980} with adaptive step, from output point to output point, with the error measured on the state scaled by (\SI{1}{\bar}, \SI{1}{\kelvin}). The reference implementation expanded $\dd\bPhi/\dd x$ term by term, evaluated the partial derivatives of the flash outputs by one-sided finite differences at the cost of three flashes per evaluation, and integrated with a library solver; here $\bJ$ is exact (Section~\ref{sec:ad}) and a single flash is needed per right-hand-side evaluation. The converged equilibrium ratios of the last accepted step are carried as an auxiliary variable and seed the next flash, which halves the substitution iterations without changing the result.

\paragraph{Finite differences} With $P$ and $T$ as unknowns at $n$ nodes per segment, the flux derivative is discretised with the second-order operator $\mathbf{M}$ (centred inside, one-sided at the ends), $(\mathbf{M}\bPhi)_i = \bS_i$. For the mechanistic model this is the nodal method of the reference, with the phase mass rates eliminated algebraically ($\mdot_G = w_G\mdot$), which keeps two unknowns per node.

\paragraph{Finite volumes} Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:flux} over each cell gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fv}
\bPhi_{i+1} - \bPhi_i = \frac{h}{2}\left(\bS_i + \bS_{i+1}\right),
\end{equation}
which is conservative by construction and never differentiates the fluxes across the kinks introduced by the dew point or by a change of flow pattern. Break points between segments are duplicated with continuity of $P$ and $T$. Both nodal systems are solved by Newton's method,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newton}
\bJ_N(\bu^{k})\,\delta\bu = -\mathbf{R}(\bu^{k}), \qquad \bu^{k+1} = \bu^{k} + \delta\bu,
\end{equation}
with the exact banded Jacobian assembled by forward-mode automatic differentiation with graph colouring (bandwidth two for the finite-difference operator, one for the finite volumes), starting from the no-slip march interpolated at the nodes. Every run reports the global balances $(\bPhi_\mathrm{out} - \bPhi_\mathrm{in}) - \int \bS\,\dd x$ per segment, evaluated on the solver's own grid, as a diagnostic.

\subsection{Automatic differentiation and vectorisation}\label{sec:ad}

The physics is written once against an injected array backend; the same code runs in NumPy, for inspection and small cases, and in JAX \citep{jax2018}, where it is compiled just in time and transformed. Three transformations matter here. First, forward-mode linearisation gives $\bJ$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:march} and the coloured Newton Jacobian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:newton} exactly, at the cost of two tangent evaluations instead of three extra flashes. Second, the flash is itself differentiable: the successive-substitution iterations are excluded from the derivative graph and the Newton polishing steps, which solve the equilibrium conditions to machine precision, carry the derivatives of the vapour fraction and of the phase compositions with respect to $P$ and $T$ by the implicit function theorem, so that the tangents are those of the converged solution and not of a truncated iteration. Functions with restricted domains (square roots, logarithms, powers) are guarded before evaluation so that the tangents stay finite, and the flash runs inside the compiled program, since an eager call would be retraced at every step. Third, the compiled march is vectorised over cases with the pipe and inlet parameters as arguments: one compilation serves any number of cases with the same fluid and grid, and a batch of 300 cases costs roughly the time of the slowest case rather than the sum. This vectorised march is the data generator of the companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b}, where the same compiled right-hand side also provides the residual of the governing equations for physics-informed surrogates.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:implementation}

Each case is a self-contained JSON document (fluid with component properties, pipe profile, inlet conditions, model and solver options) and each recorded run stores the profiles together with the case and the software provenance. The reference curves used below were digitised from the figures of \citet{carvalho2022} and its tabulated appendices were extracted from the document; every figure and table of this article is produced by one script from the recorded runs and these reference data.

\subsection{Pressure drop in horizontal and vertical lines}\label{sec:dp}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dp_parity} and Table~\ref{tab:dp_stats} compare the pressure drop in the 200 lines (\SI{100}{\metre}, adiabatic, binary CH$_4$/C$_{7+}$ fluid) with the values tabulated by the reference implementation. In the vertical lines, where the hydrostatic term dominates, the mechanistic model reproduces the reference with a median deviation of \SI{+3}{\percent} and 72\,\% of the cases within \SI{10}{\percent}; the no-slip model, which has no slip to raise the holdup, is \SI{14}{\percent} lower in the median, as expected. In the horizontal lines both models are systematically lower than the reference, by about one half. The reference itself noted that its horizontal values lay far above the five literature correlations it compared with (mean deviations of several hundred percent), whereas the present values lie below that band. The friction closures and the liquid-viscosity correlation were checked term by term against the printed equations and against the digitised property comparisons of the reference, so the origin of the horizontal discrepancy lies in an unprinted implementation detail of the reference, most likely in the stratified-flow holdup or interfacial friction, and it is left open.

\subsection{Offshore production system}\label{sec:offshore}

Fig.~\ref{fig:offshore} and Table~\ref{tab:offshore_points} show the profiles along the well, flowline and riser. The no-slip model reproduces the no-slip curves of the reference: \SI{119.98}{\bar} against \SI{119.97}{\bar} at the wellhead, within \SI{1.7}{\bar} at the riser base, within \SI{1.4}{\kelvin} in temperature everywhere, and annular flow along the whole system, as reported. Since the energy equation and its closure are shared by all cases, this agreement verifies the heat-transfer formulation that is questioned in Section~\ref{sec:condensate}. With the mechanistic model the present implementation loses more pressure along the flowline than the reference (\SI{102}{\bar} against \SI{115}{\bar} at the riser base), consistently with the horizontal lines of Section~\ref{sec:dp}. The holdup of the reference is $0.85\times$ the present value in all three segments for both models; the ratio is the same for the no-slip model, whose holdup is the flash fraction $\lambda_L$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}, so it is thermodynamic and traces to the characterisation of the C$_{7+}$ pseudo-component (molar mass \SI{560}{\gram\per\mole}, acentric factor 1.32): a liquid density about \SI{25}{\percent} higher, or a critical pressure $1.25\times$ larger, reproduces the reference curve.

\subsection{Gas-condensate pipeline}\label{sec:condensate}

The \SI{8}{\kilo\metre} pipeline with a rich gas (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}) was simulated for two inlet temperatures and three heat-transfer coefficients. With the printed values of $U$ the present model cooled the gas far more slowly than the reference figures. Fitting the exponential cooling law $T - T_\mathrm{amb} \propto \exp(-a x)$ to the single-phase part of the six reference profiles gave pure exponentials (residual below \SI{0.1}{\kelvin}) whose decay rate per unit $U$ was the same in all six cases and $3.4\times$ the value of $U\pi D/(\mdot c_p)$ with the printed data, with no additive term and no dependence on temperature. A single constant factor on the heat-loss term therefore explains the whole set, whereas an error in the thermodynamics or in the energy equation is ruled out both by the shape of the profiles and by the agreement in the offshore system; the printed $U$ values are taken as a presentation error of the reference and the effective values $3.42\,U$ are used. Fig.~\ref{fig:condensate} and Table~\ref{tab:condensate} then show agreement of the temperature profiles to \SIrange{0.1}{0.3}{\kelvin}, of the outlet pressure to \SI{0.04}{\bar} and of the onset of condensation to within \SI{1.1}{\kilo\metre}; the dew-point temperature at the onset agrees to \SI{1.5}{\kelvin}. The reference vapour-fraction curves drop discontinuously at the dew point, which the equilibrium flash cannot produce, and then flatten above the present curves: this is the signature of a dead band in the phase-stability test of the reference. The vapour fraction plotted there is molar, which the present curves match to 0.004 in the two-phase region.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Writing the compositional two-phase pipe-flow model in flux form on an array backend with automatic differentiation gave exact Jacobians and flash derivatives, three solvers that verify one another to \SI{0.01}{\bar} on the same equations, global balances that close to machine precision in the finite-volume scheme, Newton convergence in one to three iterations across phase and pattern changes, and a vectorised march that generates hundreds of converged profiles per minute. Against the reference implementation the no-slip model reproduces the offshore system and, with an effective heat-transfer coefficient identified from the shape of the reference profiles, the gas-condensate pipeline; the mechanistic model reproduces the vertical pressure drops and leaves the horizontal ones, lower than the reference by about one half, as an open question for which the unprinted closure details of the reference are the suspect. The finite-volume scheme on the flux interface is the recommended nodal method, since it is conservative, narrow-banded and insensitive to the kinks of the dew point and of the pattern transitions, while the march remains the tool of choice for generating data. The companion paper \citep{carvalho2026b} builds on both: the vectorised march generates the training sets of neural surrogates of the production system, and the compiled right-hand side supplies the residual of the governing equations to physics-informed surrogates that return whole profiles with the axial position as an input.

\section{Equation-of-state parameters}\label{app:eos}
The attractive and covolume parameters of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pr} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr_ab}
a = \Omega_a \frac{R^2 T_c^2}{P_c}, \qquad b = \Omega_b \frac{R T_c}{P_c}, \qquad \alpha(T) = \left[1 + \kappa\left(1 - \sqrt{T/T_c}\right)\right]^2, \qquad \kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226\,\omega - 0.26992\,\omega^2,
\end{equation}
with $\Omega_a = 0.45724$ and $\Omega_b = 0.0778$ as printed in the reference, and the van der Waals mixing rules with zero binary interaction parameters. The critical properties, acentric factors, normal boiling points, enthalpies of formation and ideal-gas heat-capacity polynomials of the pure components are those tabulated by \citet{carvalho2022}; the C$_{7+}$ pseudo-component of the offshore fluid is characterised by its molar mass (\SI{560}{\gram\per\mole}), critical temperature (\SI{991.4}{\kelvin}), critical pressure (\SI{8.379}{\bar}) and acentric factor (1.32), with the ideal-gas heat capacity extrapolated from the normal alkanes.
